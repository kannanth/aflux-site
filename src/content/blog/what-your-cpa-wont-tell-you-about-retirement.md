## The $10,000 Gap

You pay your CPA to file your taxes. You pay your financial advisor to manage your investments. Both are good at their jobs. But neither one is looking at the complete picture.

Your CPA knows your tax bracket but doesn't know your 401(k) balance. Your FA knows your asset allocation but doesn't know your rental property depreciation. The result? You leave money on the table every year.

Here are real examples of what falls through the gap:

## 1. The Retirement Contribution Blind Spot

Your FA says "max out your 401(k)." Your CPA says "you're in the 32% bracket." Neither tells you the optimal order:

1. 401(k) up to employer match (free money — always first)
2. HSA (triple tax advantage — deductible, grows tax-free, withdraws tax-free)
3. Roth IRA (if your MAGI allows it)
4. 401(k) to the annual limit
5. Traditional IRA (if deductible at your income level)
6. Taxable brokerage (remaining savings)

This waterfall can save thousands in taxes. But your CPA doesn't know your employer match percentage, and your FA doesn't know your MAGI phase-out status. Neither runs the calculation.

## 2. The Roth Conversion Miscalculation

Your FA suggests a Roth conversion. "Tax-free growth!" they say. But whether a conversion actually saves you money depends on comparing your current marginal tax bracket (which your CPA knows) to your projected retirement bracket (which your FA should model but often doesn't).

If you're in the 32% bracket now and expect to be in the 22% bracket in retirement, a Roth conversion costs you 10% more in taxes today. That's not always a good deal — but you'd never know without running both sides.

## 3. The Rental Property Tax Shield

You own rental properties. Your CPA tracks the depreciation for Schedule E. Your FA sees the rental income in your cash flow. But neither connects the dots:

- How does depreciation recapture affect your long-term capital gains if you sell?
- Should you do a 1031 exchange or take the tax hit?
- How does rental net income affect your retirement timeline?

These questions live at the intersection of tax planning and financial planning. Neither professional owns that intersection.

## 4. The Fund Fee Blind Spot

Your FA put you in funds that charge 0.5-1% expense ratios. Your CPA doesn't look at investment fees — that's not their domain. But here's the math:

- $500K portfolio at 0.50% ER = $2,500/year in fees
- Same funds available at 0.03% ER = $150/year
- Savings: $2,350/year
- Over 30 years (with compound drag): $180,000+

Your FA may not recommend cheaper alternatives because they earn commissions on the expensive ones. Your CPA never sees the fee schedule. You need a third perspective.
